tiny386 for the ESP32-S31-Korvo-1 (esp32-s31-linux be5b725)
===========================================================

Status
======
Added 2026-10-24: it has run on the board since this was written. On 2026-10-21
tiny386-korvo booted FreeDOS on the panel at 0.358 million guest instructions a
second, and the board had 5,076 KiB available with everything running. README.md
has the results. The rest of this file is as it was before that run.

Built with the board's toolchain and tested under qemu-riscv32-static. It has not run
on the board. No Windows has been started in it, here or anywhere: I had no Windows
media and was not to fetch any.

What has been seen, all of it under emulation:
* tiny386 as its author released it builds for the board without a warning once two
  files that are not for it are left out, and boots FreeDOS to a prompt.
* The board's binary, tiny386-korvo, boots FreeDOS 1.4 to a prompt from a hard disk
  image and from FreeDOS's own boot floppy; takes commands; and ends when FreeDOS
  switches the machine off. On a terminal, and against a fake copy of the panel's
  console, where the picture was read back off the frame buffer.
* 26 test cases, 39 deliberately broken copies, and 707 checks of the BIOS and
  the VGA, listed under "Tests".
* The BIOS files built from source: tiny386's own byte for byte, and then with
  the BIOS fixes made here (see "The BIOS").
* Two builds of each binary from nothing, in different directories, give the same
  bytes.

What is worked out and not measured: how much memory it needs, and how fast it will
be. Both are below, with what they rest on, and TEST-PLAN.txt has the commands that
turn them into measurements. The short of it:
* Memory: a guest of 4 MB needs 5.3 MB of the board's as the emulator counts it.
  Whether the board has that free with Wi-Fi up is not known.
* Speed: 130 to 310 thousand guest instructions a second, by counts of the
  emulator's own instructions and stores and the two timing figures the Wolfenstein
  3D port got from the board. That is an IBM PC or a slow AT, running 386 code.
  FreeDOS is at its prompt in about a minute at that rate. Windows 95 would take
  tens of minutes to start, which is a guess from other machines, not a count.

What it is
==========
tiny386 (https://github.com/hchunhui/tiny386), an x86 PC emulator in C written to
run Windows 9x on small microcontroller boards, at commit 9ed786b, with one patch and
a front end of its own for this board. The front end puts the picture on /dev/fb0
through the frame buffer console driver of SDL 1.2.15, the SDL that the Wolfenstein
3D port for this board patched and that has run on it. Everything is linked into one
static binary; nothing has to be installed on the board.

The emulated PC is tiny386's: a 386 or 486 without x87, SeaBIOS, a VGA with the
Bochs VBE extension, two IDE channels, a PS/2 keyboard and mouse, PIC, PIT, RTC,
DMA, an NE2000 with nothing behind it, a Sound Blaster 16 and a PC speaker that
nobody hears (the board has no sound). The Adlib synthesiser is left out of this
build: it is 140 KB of tables for a sound that goes nowhere.

Running it
==========
TEST-PLAN.txt has the steps in a careful order. In short, with the binary, the two
BIOS files, the image and conf/freedos.ini in a folder tiny386 on the card:
     cp /mnt/sd/tiny386/tiny386-korvo /root/tiny386
     chmod +x /root/tiny386
     cd /mnt/sd/tiny386
     /root/tiny386 freedos.ini
File names in an .ini are looked for from the directory the emulator is started in.

Options
=======
(none)           the guest's picture on the frame buffer console
-term            the guest's text screen on the terminal the emulator was started
                 from, drawn with that terminal's own characters; no SDL, no
                 graphics modes. For a first look, and for ssh.
-none            no screen and no keyboard: for timing
-insns N         stop after N million guest instructions
-time S          stop after S seconds
-marks           time a guest that marks its work, from mark to mark, and stop at
                 its last mark: BENCH.COM on the FreeDOS image (see "Speed")
-kips N          run no faster than N thousand instructions a second: to see on a
                 PC what a slow machine is like
-share P         at most P in 100 of the time goes on drawing the screen (20)
-fps N           at most N pictures a second (10)
-plainvga        draw the 16-colour modes pixel by pixel, like the others: slower,
                 and what to try if such a picture looks wrong
-log FILE        the emulator's and the BIOS's messages go to FILE. Without it
                 they are dropped once the emulator has started: in graphics mode
                 the console would keep them for later, on top of each other.
-nosync          do not ask for the guest's disk writes to be put on the card
-force           run even if the machine has less memory than the guest needs
--hostbench      time this machine's loads and stores, and stop
--fix-console    put the console back in text mode after a killed run

In the environment:
TINY386_NOGUARD=1      do not start the console guard
TINY386_MEMINFO=FILE   read FILE in place of /proc/meminfo: for the tests
SDL_NOMOUSE=1, SDL_FBCON_NEWVT=1, SDL_FBCON_VTSWITCH=1, SDL_VIDEODRIVER=dummy
                       as in the Wolfenstein 3D port's notes: they are SDL's

Every run ends with lines such as
     tiny386: 5003429 instructions in 21.4 s of running: 0.234 million a second
     tiny386: beside that, 0.3 s for 140 pictures (2.1 ms each, 38.0 at most), ...
     tiny386: most memory this process had in use: 2904 KiB, of which the guest's 2304
     tiny386: available memory was 6120 KiB before and is 3310 KiB now, ...
(the numbers there are made up). "Running" leaves out the time the guest was halted,
the time spent drawing and the time spent waiting for the card.

Keys
====
With the picture on the panel every key goes to the guest but one combination:
Ctrl+Alt+Backspace ends the emulator. With -term, Ctrl+] does; the function keys F1
to F5 type a letter there (the Linux console's codes for them are not ones tiny386's
terminal mode knows), and Ctrl+C goes to the guest. Ctrl+Alt+F1 and the like do not
switch consoles while the picture is up.

A mouse has to be plugged in before the emulator starts. SDL finds it among
/dev/input/event0 to event31 and says which on stderr, which is in the -log file.

The picture
===========
The VGA draws straight into the frame buffer: 16 bits a pixel, RGB565, which is what
the panel is. There is no second copy of the screen and no conversion on the way.
For that the emulator asks SDL for the whole screen, 800x480, whatever [display]
says: the VGA takes a row to be as long as the picture is wide, and a smaller
picture, which SDL would centre, has rows as long as the screen's. If [display]
says something else the emulator says so and uses the screen's size.

Inside those 800x480 the VGA centres what the guest shows and cuts off what does not
fit. tiny386 does not scale on this path.
  text, 80x25                  720x400, a border of 40 pixels all round
  320x200 in 256 colours       640x400 (each pixel twice each way), border 80 and 40
  640x480 in 16 colours        the full height, 80 black pixels either side
  640x400, 640x350             centred, like the above
  800x600 and up               the top left 800x480 of it
So for Windows: the standard VGA driver, 640x480 in 16 colours, fits the panel's
height exactly. Screen shots of the first three are what the tests check.

What a picture costs, counted for the board's code (tests/pictures-rv32.txt). The
milliseconds are the guess that "Speed" below explains, not a measurement:

                                         instructions      stores     ms
  text, 80x25
    after a change of mode                  2,584,000     196,000     68
    everything drawn again                  2,344,000       4,300     38
    nothing has changed                        37,000         320    0.6
  320x200 in 256 colours
    after a change of mode                  4,090,000     440,000    125
    nothing has changed                     3,603,000          47     58
  640x480 in 16 colours
    after a change of mode                  4,126,000     519,000    136
    everything drawn again                  3,598,000      39,000     63
    nothing has changed                       371,000         560      6
  640x480 in 16 colours, as tiny386 draws it (-plainvga)
    after a change of mode                 12,834,000     481,000    270
    nothing has changed                    12,306,000         530    197
  320x200 in 16 colours (mode 0Dh)
    after a change of mode                  2,545,000     440,000    100
    everything drawn again                  2,074,000      18,000     36
    nothing has changed                       185,000       1,700      3
  320x200 in 16 colours, pixel by pixel (-plainvga)
    after a change of mode                 10,724,000     423,000    229
    nothing has changed                    10,254,000         450    164

"After a change of mode" is the screen cleared and the picture drawn on it. Until
2026-10-19 a text screen was cleared twice then, once for the mode and once because
the text has a new size or place: 2,824,000 instructions, 388,000 stores, 98 ms. The
second clear is now left out when nothing has been drawn since the first. The two
-plainvga parts of the table are why the patch draws the 16-colour modes another
way. As released, tiny386 works out
every pixel of the screen at every picture, some 39 instructions each in a 16-colour
mode: a fifth of a second of this processor to redraw a desktop on which nothing has
moved, sixty times a second if it could. Its own boards have a second core for that.
Four things are done about it here:
* The front end draws when the guest's vertical retrace comes round, but at most ten
  times a second (-fps), and after a picture that took t it waits four times t
  before the next, so that drawing gets a fifth of the time at most (-share).
* A pixel is stored only where the screen does not already show it, with one store
  of 16 bits. Every store on this board goes out to the PSRAM; a load does not.
* In the 16-colour modes the four bytes that hold eight pixels in the four planes
  are one aligned word of video memory. That word is kept for every eight pixels of
  the picture, and at the next picture eight pixels whose word is what it was are
  passed over. In mode 0Dh, at half the dot clock, each pixel is two dots wide, so
  one word is sixteen pixels of the screen. This takes 150 KB while 640x480 is up,
  62 KB in mode 0Dh. tests/vgatest.c holds it against the old way; see "Tests".
* tiny386 clears a graphics screen at every write to a register of the CRT
  controller, and the BIOS makes two such writes for every character it prints: it
  moves the text cursor, which a graphics mode does not show (counted: VGA12T's
  forty characters are 82 writes more than VGA12 makes). A clear and the
  picture drawn on it again is the "after a change of mode" line of the table: 136
  ms, or 270 as tiny386 draws it, and a black flash, for each character. Here the
  clear waits for the next picture, and is left out when that picture is where the
  last one was with black round it, which is when clearing and drawing would leave
  the same screen. The same test holds this against the old way, and VGA12T shows
  it.
-plainvga turns the last two off, and the second text clear left out above: the
16-colour modes are then drawn, and cleared, as tiny386 does it.
The 256-colour modes and the VBE modes still have every pixel looked at each time,
14 instructions each: about 58 ms for 640x400, so three or four pictures a second.
Mode 0Dh (320x200 in 16 colours, half the dot clock) went that way too until
2026-10-19, at 164 ms for every picture whether anything had changed or not; the
quick way takes it now. It has a loop for each dot clock and tells the compiler
which is the likely one, so that 640x480 costs what it did (500 instructions more a
picture, counted). Written as one loop for both, a still 640x480 picture cost nearly
three times as much; as two loops without the hint, 7,000 instructions and 1,400
stores more.

The picture is written straight into the visible screen, with no second page and
no waiting for the panel, so a picture that is half drawn can be seen as such.

Memory
======
None of this is measured on the board. Under emulation the kernel's view of a
process cannot be had, so this is from the emulator's system calls (qemu -strace),
the sizes in the binary, and the sources.

What the emulator takes, for a guest with M of memory and V of video memory:

  the guest's memory        M       all of it at once: the emulator clears it at
                                    the start, which makes the kernel provide it
  the guest's video memory  V       likewise
  the emulator's heap       152 KiB asked of the kernel in a run on the fake console
                                    (48 KiB of it is the NE2000's buffer)
  16-colour modes           150 KiB while one is up: the words described above
  static data               up to 164 KiB, of which SDL's key maps are 128 that the
                            patched driver mostly does not touch: say 30
  stack                     a few pages: say 16 KiB
  code                      575 KiB in the file, read from the card as it is used
                            and given back by the kernel if memory is short: say
                            300 KiB in use
  the console guard         a second process that sleeps: its stack, and what the
                            kernel keeps for any process: say 50 KiB
  the frame buffer          none: it is the panel's own 752 KiB, there already

That is M + V + 0.55 MiB in a text mode and M + V + 0.7 MiB in a 16-colour mode, of
which only M, V and the 152 KiB are counted and the rest is estimate. The emulator
allows itself 1 MiB when it checks, and prints the real figure (the kernel's VmHWM
for the process) at the end of every run.

Against the board: 16 MiB of PSRAM, of which Linux has 15,376 KiB. What is free of
that with the system up is not known; the working guess has been 5 to 7 MiB with
Wi-Fi running, never measured.

  guest                               needs, as the emulator checks it
  FreeDOS, 2M + 256K                  3.25 MiB
  4M + 256K                           5.25 MiB     fits the guess, just
  6M + 320K  (tiny386's 800x480 board, Windows 95)
                                      7.3 MiB      does not, without making room
  7M + 768K  (tiny386's win98.ini)    8.75 MiB     does not
  7568K + 320K  (tiny386's first board, Windows 95)
                                      8.7 MiB      does not

The emulator reads MemAvailable from /proc/meminfo before it takes anything and
stops if the guest and its megabyte are more than that: it would otherwise be
killed by the kernel in the middle of starting, or, with the 64 MiB swap file, live
on with part of the guest in the swap file, where one guest instruction can cost a
read from the card. -force overrides it. MemAvailable does not count what other
processes could give up to the swap file, so there is some slack beyond it: idle
daemons swapped out cost nothing, the guest swapped out costs everything.

Making room: TEST-PLAN.txt, step 1, measures what stopping the Wi-Fi (wpa_supplicant,
wpa_cli, udhcpc), dropbear, crond, klogd and syslogd gives back. I do not know the
figure; one to three megabytes is my guess. Stopping the Wi-Fi does not unload its
driver, and its firmware is in the chip's own SRAM, not in the PSRAM.

Windows 95 was sold as needing 4 MB and is not pleasant with less than 8. With 4M
it will page to its swap file inside the disk image all the time; whether that is
bearable at this speed is the open question, more than whether it starts.

Speed
=====
Counted, not timed. qemu's own speed says nothing about the board, so the emulator's
work was counted instead: tests/guest/bench.S is a DOS program that does nine fixed
pieces of work and marks where it is; tests/perf runs it on the board's emulator
(the objects of tiny386-korvo with another main()) under qemu-riscv32-static, which
logs every block of instructions it carries out; tests/tracecount counts them between
the marks. For one guest instruction, on the board's code as built (-O3):

                                         host     loads   stores    thousand
                                         instr.                     a second
  16-bit arithmetic in registers          89.7     22.5    13.5       307
  16-bit loads and stores                168.9     40.6    20.1       185
  16-bit calls and stack                 119.7     30.4    14.2       261
  DOS calls (int 21h)                    139.3     35.7    17.3       219
  32-bit arithmetic, paging on            87.2     22.6    13.5       310
  32-bit loads and stores, paging on     179.7     45.6    21.4       173
  32-bit calls and stack, paging on      140.3     36.8    15.5       231
  a translation cache miss every time    197.1     53.1    27.2       147
  rep movsw and rep stosw, for each BYTE  18.8      3.8     1.26     2,120

The last column is the guess. The Wolfenstein 3D port timed three frame rates on the
board and counted instructions and stores for the same three; two of the rates fix
two figures, 16 ns for an instruction and 135 ns for a store, and the third came out
within 3 in 100. With those, a guest instruction costs 3 to 7 microseconds here,
about half of it in stores: the emulator stores 13 to 27 times for every guest
instruction (the instruction pointer twice for each byte it fetches, five words for
the flags, the count of instructions, and its own stack).

Two cautions about that column. The two figures were fitted with counts of x86 code
(valgrind), and x86 stores more than RV32 does for the same C; tests/perf-x86.txt is
this benchmark counted that way, and comes to 144 to 298 thousand a second, the same
band. And the figures are from a game; an emulator may sit differently in the
caches. So: 130 to 310 thousand instructions a second, and I would not be surprised
by half or double. -marks on the board settles it (TEST-PLAN.txt, step 6): it times
the same nine pieces.

What that is like: an IBM PC did about 250 thousand instructions a second and an
8 MHz AT about a million, both of 16-bit code. Held to 250 thousand a second on a PC
(-kips 250), the emulator has FreeDOS at its prompt 37 seconds after starting, and
"dir" takes 6 seconds; held to 120 thousand, 73 and 13 seconds. Nothing in the BIOS
or in FreeDOS minded being that slow. Starting FreeDOS is about nine million
instructions.

For Windows 95 I have no count. A 386DX-33 does some five million instructions a
second and takes a minute or two to start it; at a twentieth to a fortieth of that
rate, with more paging because there is less memory, half an hour is the kind of
figure to expect, and it is a guess.

Why -O3: tiny386 builds itself with -O3, and the counts agree. The same benchmark
built -O2 has the three pieces with paging at 207, 172 and 212 instructions and
27.5, 22.4 and 30.7 stores: a fifth to a third slower by the guess, because at -O2
the address translation is a function call with its own stack frame. The 16-bit
pieces are the same either way, and the binary is 24 KB larger. -Os was not better
anywhere. What -O3 does to the 16 KB instruction cache, counts cannot say.

What was not done: the emulator's inner loop is as released. The stores are in its
design (two counters for the instruction pointer, the flags kept as five words) and
taking them out is a rewrite of the loop, not a change that can be shown to be safe
by looking at it.

Unaligned loads and stores
==========================
tiny386 reads and writes guest memory through pointers that are as aligned as the
guest's addresses, and says so: "only works on hosts that are little-endian and
support unaligned access". x86 code does not align much. Counted on a PC, while the
BIOS and FreeDOS start, one guest instruction in eight makes the emulator load or
store 16 or 32 bits at an address that is not a multiple of that size; in the
benchmark's pieces with calls, one in thirteen.

Whether the ESP32-S31 does such an access itself I could not find out. The port's
kernel is configured as if they work and are slow, its firmware is OpenSBI, which
does them by hand when the processor traps, and nothing in the port's notes says.
If the processor traps, every one of them is a trip into the firmware, some hundreds
of instructions, and the emulator would be several times slower than the figures
above with nothing to show why.

So tiny386-korvo is built with -DI386_ALIGNED_ONLY: a load or a store that is not
aligned is made of smaller ones that are (unaligned.h). The cost where it was not
needed is small: the aligned case has one test more. That the build makes no
unaligned access was checked on a PC with gcc's alignment sanitizer, through
FreeDOS, the benchmark and the three kinds of picture: no report, where the same
run of the other build reports all four of its accessors. tiny386-korvo-ua is that
other build, with the accessors as released. --hostbench times an aligned and an
unaligned load and store on the machine it is run on and says which build suits.

The console
===========
With the picture up, the panel's console is in graphics mode and its keyboard in raw
mode. What puts them back, and when:
* Any ordinary end (the guest switches off, Ctrl+Alt+Backspace, -time, -insns,
  -marks), and any error the emulator stops on: SDL, before the closing lines or
  the complaint are printed.
* A signal that ends the emulator (TERM, INT, HUP, SEGV, ABRT and the rest): SDL's
  frame buffer driver, as patched for the Wolfenstein 3D port, in the signal handler.
  "killall tiny386" from another login is safe. No closing lines then.
* The emulator killed outright (kill -9, or the kernel when memory runs out): the
  console guard, a second process started before anything is changed. It holds one
  end of a pipe and sleeps until the other end closes, which it does when the
  emulator is gone however that happened; then it puts back what it finds. It is the
  same binary started with --console-guard, and the same idea as that port's guard,
  written again for this program.
* By hand: /root/tiny386 --fix-console.
With -term the terminal is in raw mode and on its second screen, which the guard
also puts right when the terminal is a console; over ssh "reset" does.

The emulator cannot be hung by a guest: it leaves the guest every 4,096
instructions to look at the clock, the keyboard and the screen. A guest that has
stopped is still an emulator that listens.

"killall -9 tiny386" kills the guard as well: BusyBox finds processes by the file
they run, and the guard runs the same file.

Disks
=====
A disk is a file: 16 heads, 63 sectors a track, as many cylinders as it is long.
The emulator hands every write to the kernel as the guest makes it, so an emulator
that is killed loses nothing of its own; the kernel caches the file in whatever
memory is free, and a write is on the card only some seconds later. When the guest
has written and then been quiet for two seconds the emulator asks the kernel to put
the image's data on the card and waits for it (-nosync turns that off), and it does
the same when it ends. Floppy images are treated the same way. A board that loses
power in between loses what was not yet written, and a guest's file system may not
survive that. Keep a copy of an image that took an evening to make.

A floppy image has to be the size of a floppy that tiny386 knows (360K, 720K, 1.2M,
1.44M, 2.88M and a few more): the emulator says so before it starts if it is not.

enable_serial = 1 is refused. tiny386's serial port is the emulator's standard
input, a byte at a time: on this board that is the keyboard, and the guest would
get every key, the ones that end the emulator among them.

What the patch changes
======================
The patch begins with this list, file by file; in short:
* I386_ALIGNED_ONLY (i386.c, pc.c, vga.c, new unaligned.h).
* VGA_STORE_CHANGED, the quick way for 16-colour modes (mode 0Dh too), the clear of
  a graphics screen put off until the next picture, and the second clear of a text
  screen left out when nothing has been drawn since the first (vga.c, vga.h).
* PC_STEP_COUNT and PC_DEBUGCON can be set when building (pc.c). The board's build
  has the emulator leave the guest every 4,096 instructions, not every 10,240, and
  the BIOS's debug messages go where the emulator's do.
* ide_write_count() and ide_flush(), emulink_write_count() and emulink_flush(), and
  every write to an image handed to the kernel at once (ide.c, misc.c). These last
  are not behind a build flag: a write to a disk image is followed by an fflush(),
  and two stores into an ATAPI answer are made byte by byte, in every build.
* Ctrl+] in the terminal mode can be given to the front end (term.c).
* korvo/main.c, guard.c, hostbench.c: the front end, new.
* What the VGA does that tiny386 did not, found by the BIOS tests (vga.c, pc.c):
  the split screen, horizontal panning, the preset row scan, mode 0Dh at its full
  width, 40-column text, text rows from the top of the picture, blinking, the
  underline, a tall cursor, the PEL mask, video memory shut off by the misc output
  register, VBE keeping video memory when only the DAC's width changes, the
  monochrome CRT controller ports, and the picture's address wrapping at 256 KB as
  a VGA's does (it read past the end of video memory before). These are for every
  build. BIOS-COVERAGE.txt has the details.
Apart from those, nothing changes in a build that does not ask: the other front
ends, built with their own flags, are as they were. 2,312 lines added, 83 removed,
16 files.

What turned up in tiny386 itself
================================
* "gcc -O3 *.c -o tiny386_headless -lm", as its README has it, does not build on a
  Linux that is not x86: kvm.c is among the files, and wants x86's KVM. Without it
  and amd64.c (the 64-bit guest, not wanted here) the rest builds for RV32 with
  GCC 15.3 and musl without one warning, and boots FreeDOS.
* The first picture of a text screen after a change of mode is drawn black on black:
  the colours are worked out one picture later. One frame, on a PC.
* Its terminal mode prints the BIOS's debug messages into the guest's screen
  (stdout). The board's build sends them to the log.
None of these is a fault on its own boards. Found later, by the BIOS tests and a
review, and fixed in the patch:
* The VGA lacked the split screen, panning, the preset row scan, blinking and the
  rest listed under "What the patch changes", and drew mode 0Dh as the left half of
  its picture.
* A picture whose start address is near the end of video memory was read on past
  the end of it, by as much as the rest of the picture: a guest could make the
  emulator read memory that is not video memory. The address now wraps at 256 KB,
  as a VGA's does, and pc.c puts 16 KB of slack behind video memory for the end of
  a line.
* The text cursor was drawn from a 32-byte glyph read 4 bytes a line, so a cursor
  taller than 8 lines read past its end.
* SeaVGABIOS, as tiny386 ships it, sets mode 07h by writing the monochrome CRT
  controller before it switches the misc output to monochrome, so on a VGA, real
  or emulated, mode 07h stays black. Fixed in the BIOS built here, with the other
  faults of the BIOS that the tests found: see "The BIOS".

The BIOS
========
bios.bin and vgabios.bin are SeaBIOS and SeaVGABIOS built here: SeaBIOS at 81ec9ec0
with tiny386's own patch and configuration for it, as tiny386's CI builds them, and
then patches/seabios-korvo.patch, which fixes what the BIOS tests found wrong in the
BIOS itself. tools/build-bios.sh does all of it (see "Build").

The files tiny386 released were built again from source first. With the compiler
its CI used (Ubuntu 26.04's gcc 15.2.0-16ubuntu1 and binutils 2.46, which the files
name) and the CI's build time and host name in the version string, both came out
the same bytes as the two in tiny386's release of 2026-10-08; UPSTREAM=1 makes that
build. With Debian 12's gcc 12.2 they come out different (vgabios.bin 512 bytes
longer); the exact match made testing those unnecessary.

What the patch fixes; its head says more, and BIOS-COVERAGE.txt has the tests:
* Mode 07h: the screen was black and the next mode's palette askew. SeaVGABIOS
  wrote the CRT controller at 3B4h before it switched the misc output to
  monochrome, and reset the attribute flip-flop at 3DAh, which a VGA does not
  answer in mode 07h.
* 350 scan lines (12h BL=30h) were accepted and then ignored. Text modes are now
  set in 350 lines when asked, as on IBM's VGA (80 columns 640x350, 07h 720x350),
  and 8x8 then gives 43 lines.
* Cursor emulation: 0607h is lines 13 and 14 of 16, as on a VGA (it was 14 and 15).
* 12h BL=31h (no default palette), BL=33h (summing to grey) and BL=36h (screen
  off) were accepted and did not work; they do.
* 1Bh gives the mode's rows, colours, pages and scan lines.
* 0Ch and 0Dh take the page in modes 0Dh to 10h.
* 13h AH=18h (the media type for a format) works on floppy drives.
Left as they were: 4F0Ah (VBE's protected mode interface, not a small job), and
the floppy port's faults, which are tiny386's (BIOS-COVERAGE.txt says why).

The files are not tiny386's bytes, and say so: their version string is
"rel-1.17.0-20-g81ec9ec0-dirty-20261019_000000-korvo", where the time and the host
name are fixed by the script so that every build gives the same bytes. vgabios.bin
is 40,448 bytes, 1 KB more than tiny386's; tiny386 reads it into memory at C0000h
and sets no limit there.

Toolchain
=========
The board's own: Buildroot 2026.08, GCC 15.3.0, riscv32-buildroot-linux-musl,
musl 1.2.6, rv32imac, ilp32 (soft float). tiny386's sources are built -O3 and SDL
-O2, as the Wolfenstein 3D port builds it.

Until 2026-10-14 the compiler was borrowed from the finished build of the board's
system in ~/esp32-s31-linux-main. That day "make clean" had been run in both board
build trees (the shell's history has it), which removes build/ and the compiler with
it. Nothing was put back into those trees. toolchain.sh copies the tree, with its
Buildroot submodule, and runs Buildroot's "toolchain" target in the copy: ten minutes
and 0.9 GB, in ~/tiny386-scratch/toolchain. That it is the same compiler was checked
the only way that means anything: the Wolfenstein 3D port's build.sh, pointed at it,
gave wolf3d-korvo with sha256 fbb3ab87...abdb743, which is the binary that ran on the
board at 35.3 frames a second, byte for byte.

Build (WSL2 Debian)
===================
     bash build.sh [WORKDIR]
     VARIANT=ua bash build.sh [WORKDIR]
WORKDIR defaults to ~/tiny386-scratch/build and build-ua. It needs curl, git, patch
and make, and the board's compiler where the top of the script says it looks. It
fetches SDL's tarball and checks it, clones tiny386 at the commit, applies the two
patches, builds SDL into WORKDIR/prefix and the emulator against it, and leaves
WORKDIR/tiny386-korvo. Two minutes.

Each was built twice from nothing, in different directories, and gave the same file
both times: the checksums at the end.

     bash tools/build-bios.sh [WORKDIR]
     UPSTREAM=1 bash tools/build-bios.sh [WORKDIR]
builds bios.bin and vgabios.bin into WORKDIR/out (WORKDIR defaults to
~/tiny386-scratch/bios-build), with seabios-korvo-src.tar.xz, the source tree as it
was built. It needs git, patch, make, python3, curl and xz, and for the compiler it
uses by default dpkg-deb, patchelf and unshare. It fetches the 22 Ubuntu 26.04
packages of tiny386's CI compiler (47 MB, each checked against the checksum in the
script), clones SeaBIOS at the commit, applies the patches and builds as tiny386's
CI does: two or three minutes, most of it the fetching. No root is needed. Ubuntu's
gcc calls its assembler and linker as /usr/bin/x86_64-linux-gnu-as and -ld, which
on Debian are Debian's older ones, so the script runs the build in a user and mount
namespace of its own (unshare) with Ubuntu's mounted over those two paths; nothing
outside it sees them. SeaBIOS writes the time and the host's name into its version
string; the script fixes both, so every build gives the same bytes.
UPSTREAM=1 leaves out seabios-korvo.patch and uses the time and host name that are
in tiny386's files: that build is the two files of tiny386's release of
2026-10-08, byte for byte, and the script says so. TOOLCHAIN=host uses the
system's own gcc instead (Debian 12: gcc 12.2, binutils 2.40): other bytes, and
the same results in tests/bios-tests.sh.
Two builds from nothing, in different directories, gave the same three files.

     bash tools/make-freedos-image.sh
makes the FreeDOS image: it builds the same patched sources for the PC, and has that
emulator boot FreeDOS's boot floppy and run FDISK, FORMAT and SYS on an empty disk,
typing at it through tools/drive.py. Then it copies a few of the floppy's programs
and the four of tests/guest onto the disk with mtools, and boots it.

Tests (under qemu-riscv32-static, not on the board)
===================================================
     bash tests/run-tests.sh
needs the trees that build.sh leaves, the FreeDOS image, the boot floppy that
make-freedos-image.sh fetched, the fake console of the Wolfenstein 3D port's tests
(../wolf3d/tests/fakefb.c and fbtool.py), gcc with as and ld, mtools and python3.
Four minutes. tests/results.txt is the last run for tiny386-korvo and
tests/results-ua.txt for tiny386-korvo-ua: 26 cases each, all passed.

The board's binary as it is:
* usage; a BIOS file or a disk image that is not there, a serial port asked for,
  and a floppy image of no known size are said before anything starts; too little
  memory is said, with the numbers, and -force goes on
* -none with -insns, -time and -kips; the BIOS's and the emulator's messages are in
  the -log file; -marks times BENCH.COM's nine pieces and ends
* --hostbench runs to its end
* -term on a pseudo terminal: FreeDOS from the hard disk image to C:\>, ver, mem
  (2,048K), dir, and "off", which ends the emulator; BENCH with 2M says that it
  wants 4; Ctrl+] ends it; FreeDOS from freedos.org's boot floppy to A:\>, ver, mem
  These use conf/freedos.ini and conf/freedos-floppy.ini as they are handed over.

Against the fake console (an 800x480 RGB565 frame buffer with one mode, virtual
terminals, a keyboard), with SDL's frame buffer driver and this port's guard compiled
against it unchanged and every other object the one in the board's binary:
* FreeDOS to its prompt on the screen. The text is read back off the frame buffer by
  matching every character cell against the VGA's font (tests/fbtext.py): the prompt,
  and mem's answer after "ver" and "mem" were typed at the fake keyboard as the
  kernel's key codes. The text screen begins 40 pixels in.
* A [display] of another size is put right, and said.
* VGA13: a picture of 640x400 at (80,40). VGA12: fifteen colours, and what is not
  black is 640x476 from (80,4): the bands under the black one, and the box in it.
* VGA12T, where the BIOS prints forty characters on that picture and writes to the
  CRT controller for each: run once as built and once with -plainvga, which draws
  and clears as tiny386 does. The two screens have to be the same in every pixel,
  and not the screen of VGA12.
* Ctrl+Alt+Backspace, the guest switching off, SIGTERM, kill -9 with the guard and
  without it, --fix-console, and too little memory: after each the console has to be
  as it was found, by the steps that put it back; nothing may be printed while it is
  in graphics mode; and with too little memory the console is never touched.

     gcc -O2 -std=gnu11 -I. -DBPP=16 -DVGA_STORE_CHANGED -o vgatest \
         .../tests/vgatest.c vgafont.c && ./vgatest 6000
in the patched tree, on a PC: the quick way of drawing the 16-colour modes against
the old one. vga.c is compiled into the test as it is. Registers, palette and video
memory are set at random: what the BIOS sets for mode 12h in half of the rounds, for
mode 0Dh in a quarter and for text in a quarter, then some of it changed (noise in
the video memory in half of the rounds, a few values over and over in the other
half, as a desktop is). A picture is drawn, then eleven more after small changes,
among them writes to the CRT controller's registers through the guest's ports: the
same value again, the text cursor's, any of them, the size; a split screen moved, or
the picture panned, which the quick way has to stand back for; the dot clock halved
or put back, text turned to graphics or back, the screen blanked or shown again. A
second VGA goes through the same with the quick way off, clearing its screen at
every such write as tiny386 does and clearing a text screen twice after a change of
mode, and the two screens have to match in every pixel after every picture, on four
sizes of screen (one an odd number of pixels wide, which cuts the last pixel of mode
0Dh in half). In a run of 6,000 rounds that is 72,000 pictures, about 27,000 of them
drawn the quick way from top to bottom and 11,000 of those at half the dot clock
(panning and the other modes keep it out of the rest), 37,000 writes to the CRT
controller, 9,500 pictures with a clear put off and 6,500 of those clears left out,
20,000 text pictures and 700 second clears of a text screen left out. Three such
runs with different seeds and one under gcc's address and undefined-behaviour
sanitizers: no difference, no report. Then 39 broken copies (planes in the wrong
order, the quick way not standing back for panning, bits in the wrong order, the
palette not compared, the size or the dot clock not compared, "draw everything"
ignored, the words not kept, a pixel of mode 0Dh drawn once or past the edge of the
screen, a clear that does not make the quick way forget, a clear left out when the
rest of the screen is not black or the picture has moved, a second text clear left
out after text or something else was drawn, and so on): each one has to fail in
1,200 rounds, and does. Five of them draw the right picture and only lose the
saving; the test fails those because it counts what it saw and finds no clear left
out, no second text clear left out, or no picture drawn the quick way at half the
dot clock.

The alignment check is described under "Unaligned loads and stores".

Not yet checked on the board
============================
Everything. In particular:
* That the binary starts at all: that the board's kernel and processor take what the
  emulator and qemu took.
* How much memory there is, and what the emulator really takes (VmHWM).
* The speed: every figure in "Speed" and in the table of pictures.
* Whether the processor does unaligned loads and stores itself.
* The picture on the real panel: its place, its colours, tearing, how it looks while
  the guest scrolls.
* The real keyboard through the real kernel's key codes; the fake one sent what the
  kernel's source says it sends. The lock keys' lights will not follow.
* A mouse. SDL's reading of /dev/input/eventN ran on the board in Wolfenstein 3D;
  the way from there to the guest's PS/2 mouse has not been tried anywhere, since
  FreeDOS on this image loads no mouse driver.
* The console guard on the real console, and its race with the shell.
* What the shortest sleep is on this kernel (it has no high-resolution timers, so
  10 ms is likely): a halted guest wakes that often. --hostbench times it.
* Disk images on the card: speed, and what the two-second rule costs.
* Windows, altogether.
